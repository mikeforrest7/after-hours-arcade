Recommendation: build the prototype, do not commit to commercial production yet

Build one hand-built prototype trackball station. Do not fund 10/50/100-unit tooling, do not open the donor-integration service to paying strangers, and do not price or discuss a licensed turnkey cabinet with anyone — until the conditions in §10 Kill criteria and go/no-go are met.

This is a deliberate conclusion from the numbers below, not an incomplete analysis. Three things drive it:

  1. At the $2,199 test price, the hardware-only cases yield 16.4%, 18.5% and 19.5% unit contribution for 10/50/100 units. Reaching 20% requires about $2,309 / $2,246 / $2,214 respectively. Those hardware-only prices are only $190–285 below the $2,499 Fore! Remastered factory preorder, which includes a game, display and compute. That is price pressure, not proof of current shipment or an equivalent four-game solution.
  2. The donor-integration service's first real job is not self-funding — an earlier draft's claim to the contrary is withdrawn. Its $90 "NRE" figure was an amortized 1/10th share of a $900 standalone tooling investment, not the actual cash a first job requires. Funded at its real $900 standalone cost, the job's batch-fully-loaded margin is negative at every tested stress level (0%, +15%, +30% — see §4–§5), and a conservative launch-liquidity estimate shows a $5,577.91 funding gap, not "$0 cash-at-risk" (see §6).
  3. Demand has not been validated in this work. No customer research, campaign, order collection or pilot sale was performed. Batch quantities are manufacturing scenarios, not forecasts. Used cabinets, repair/restoration services, licensed home products and doing nothing remain alternatives; a different game generation does not prove an uncontested market.

Donor integration is a separately scoped service hypothesis for owners of legitimate original hardware. It does not escape competition, software-rights review, custody risk or tooling cost. Qualify each job before accepting customer equipment.

1. What is actually sold, and what is not

Three offers were modeled because their scope, rights dependencies, liability and economics differ. None is commercially released; hardware-only carries the fewest game-distribution dependencies.

1a. Hardware-only trackball control-station kit — fewest unresolved IP questions, not a clean bill of legal health

Includes: flat-pack 18 mm plywood furniture shell (Station A, 760×610×950 mm, from arcade/cad/parameters.json), a pre-wired Ultimarc U-Trak 3 in trackball + I-PAC Ultimate I/O with harness mounted in a production-released control-deck cutout, an eight-button panel with a swappable (not game-specific) label overlay, a low-voltage audio set with rated external power, a ventilation set, a UL/ETL-listed power-distribution set, USB/data cabling with strain relief, assembly hardware, a printed assembly/service manual, and a 1-year limited hardware warranty on the furniture and included electronics.

Does NOT include, ever, at any price: a television of any kind (the customer supplies and independently brackets their own — this project's own hardware research is explicit that wall-mounting is a separate, structurally-anchored job, not a cabinet shelf); a PC, emulator, frontend, or OS; any game software, ROM, disk image, BIOS, activation key, or donor PCB; guns or holsters (this SKU is the trackball station only — the shooter console has no price model in this document, see §10); any claim of compatibility with a specific licensed game; any positional/mounted-gun hardware, recoil, or motion.

The customer supplies their lawful runtime and content. The hardware-only model therefore contains no game-distribution royalty, not a promise of zero IP exposure. Verify component licenses/certifications, applicable consumer-product rules and the assembled product's electrical/EMC requirements. A CPSC GCC is required only where an applicable rule requires it; a listed power supply does not certify the assembly. Qualified compliance advice and appropriate insurance remain release gates.

1b. Bespoke donor-system integration — not an uncontested market

The customer already owns, or independently sources, a legitimate donor arcade system: a real Silver Strike Nighthawk kit, a legacy Eagle-era Big Buck PCB set, or a 2015 Raw Thrills Jurassic Park Arcade electronics/I-O/dongle assembly. We build or rebuild a furniture shell around their hardware and bench-test it. We do not supply, copy, or modify any game software, ROM, BIOS, or security/identity device, and we do not promise or warrant the legality, completeness, or functionality of the customer's donor system beyond "it demonstrably worked immediately before and after our integration work."

This adds bailment exposure — temporary custody of hardware that may be irreplaceable — which is why it carries a higher damage reserve (7% vs. the hardware kit's 5% warranty reserve) and requires a signed waiver/scope-of-work before any customer hardware enters the shop (kill criterion K4).

The bounded research found no home-specific 2015 Jurassic Park Arcade edition. Arcade1Up's observed hunting package covers Pro/Safari-family releases, not automatically the early Eagle candidates. These are release-selection distinctions, not proof of a profitable service gap or absence of substitutes. A bespoke integration job must compete with used original cabinets, authorized alternatives and repair shops on the customer's actual requirements.

1c. Licensed turnkey cabinet — blocked, modeled for transparency only, rights chain not confirmed complete

A complete cabinet with display, PC, and a specific licensed game, ready to play. This is what Mike's original four-game vision implies for a sellable product, and it cannot be priced, built, or sold without a signed license/royalty agreement with the applicable rights holder — Incredible Technologies for Golden Tee/Silver Strike IP, Raw Thrills/Play Mechanix for Big Buck Hunter/Jurassic Park Arcade IP. No such agreement exists. These companies are each title's arcade-cabinet licensor of record, not a confirmed-complete rights chain — Jurassic Park Arcade in particular sits on top of the separately-held Jurassic Park film property, and no confirmation was found that Raw Thrills/Play Mechanix alone can grant all rights needed for a new licensed home SKU; "the rights holder" may in practice be more than one party. No public rate card for this class of small-manufacturer licensing deal was found (general royalty/minimum-guarantee mechanisms exist in licensing literature, but no figure applicable at 10–100-unit scale). This offer is modeled in economics.json/economics.csv purely to show its cost structure and make the blocking gap explicit — not because it is close to ready, and not as a claim that the two named companies are the complete rights-holder chain.

4. Unit economics — baseline

All figures from economics.py at $0 direct-cost stress (baseline). Two margin figures are reported for every scenario and are never summed or substituted for one another — see the model note at the top of economics.py for the full reasoning:

  • Marginal contribution margin = price − variable unit costs (material, labor, packaging, freight) − variable selling costs (payment-processing fee, warranty/damage reserve, CAC). Excludes one-time tooling (NRE). This is the figure break-even uses.
  • Batch-fully-loaded margin = marginal margin − this batch's own standalone NRE spread per unit. Answers "if this exact batch/job were the first-ever commercial launch and sold completely at this price, what did each unit actually net after paying for its own tooling" — not a claim about full business profit (annual operating overhead like insurance is still not in it; see §6), and not a break-even input.

Corrected framing: every scenario's nreTotalUsd below is now a STANDALONE figure — the full tooling investment that batch size (or the donor job) would need if run as the first-ever commercial launch, with nothing previously tooled. An earlier draft mixed this: it gave batch_50/batch_100 only their incremental tooling cost (assuming batch_10's jigs already existed) while still treating the shared launch-cost pool as unrecovered for every scenario, which understated batch_50/batch_100's own cost and overstated how "proven" they were relative to batch_10. The donor-integration job's nreTotalUsd similarly moved from an amortized $90/job share of a $900 tool to the real $900 standalone figure — see the "Reading the donor-integration line" note below.

6. Cash, launch liquidity, and deposit timing

This section replaces an earlier draft's "cash at risk" framing, which has been withdrawn. That framing netted customer deposits against an incomplete cash figure — it omitted CAC, payment-processing fees, the warranty/damage reserve, and the launch-cost pools — and, for the donor-integration job, concluded "self-funding... $0 cash-at-risk... the strongest number in this entire document." That conclusion was wrong and is withdrawn; see below for the corrected figures.

Assumptions (economics.json → sharedAssumptions): 50% customer deposit at order, balance before shipment; full supplier prepayment at every volume (no trade credit assumed — net terms are a future negotiation target, not assumed here); one part-time solo builder, 25 hr/week capacity, 14-day procurement lead before any build starts.

Two cash figures now, read together, never netted into a single "risk-free" number:

  • launchLiquidityRequiredUsd — a conservative floor on the cash the owner must be able to deploy for this scenario, assuming ZERO customer deposits have landed yet. It sums: the one-time launch-cost pool (business registration + website + CPSC/compliance filing, ~$2,950 mid) + the first-year annual operating cost (liability insurance, ~$1,850 mid — this recurs every year the business operates, it is not a one-time launch cost) + this scenario's own batch material/labor/packaging/freight/tooling cash (cashOutlayExclLaborUsd/cashOutlayInclLaborUsd) + customer-acquisition cash (CAC × units) + payment-processing cash (channel fee × units) + the warranty/damage reserve (a stated amount that must be held back, not spent as profit). It is a total-exposure floor, not a dated week-by-week cash-flow timeline — real spending and real deposit collection do not happen on the same day, and this figure does not model that timing; treat it as "cash that must exist," not a schedule.
  • customerDepositCashInUsd / refundableDepositLiabilityUsd — the same dollar figure, shown under two names on purpose: a collected 50% deposit is a refundable customer liability until the unit ships, not already-earned revenue the owner can spend freely.
  • fundingGapIfDepositsCollectedUsd = max(0, launch liquidity required − deposits collected) is an optimistic funding-gap calculation that assumes every deposit has already landed before any of the required cash must be spent. It is a funding-gap calculation, not a statement of zero risk — even where it nets to $0 or less, the deposit money is still a liability that must be reserved, not profit the owner can spend, and launch costs (insurance, registration, tooling) are typically paid before most real orders close in practice.

What changed from the earlier draft, and why it matters:

  1. The donor-integration job is not self-funding. The earlier draft compared only the job's own materials cash ($795) against the deposit ($1,147) and called the difference "$0 cash-at-risk... the strongest number in this entire document." That comparison dropped the $900 standalone tooling investment, the ~$2,950 one-time launch-cost pool, the ~$1,850 first-year insurance estimate, CAC, payment-processing fees, and the damage reserve — all real cash the business needs regardless of how one job's deposit nets against its own materials. Counting all of it, a single first job shows a $5,577.91 funding gap, not $0.
  2. Launch liquidity, not "cash at risk," is the right framing for a go/no-go decision. launchLiquidityRequiredUsd is deliberately not netted against deposits by default, because deposits are a liability (point 3 below) and because launch costs are typically paid before most real orders close. The optimistic netted view (fundingGapIfDepositsCollectedUsd) is shown separately and labeled as optimistic, not as the headline number.
  3. A deposit is a refundable liability, not free cash. If a batch or job fails to ship — a tooling failure, a design defect found late, the owner simply stopping — every dollar of customerDepositCashInUsd is owed back. Any net funding gap calculated against it does not erase that liability; it states what cash a prudent owner would still need to have on hand even in the optimistic case.
  4. Solo capacity, not infinite capacity, sets batch lead time. 100 units at a part-time solo pace is ~27 weeks of production — over six months before the last unit in the batch ships. Scaling past ~10 units credibly requires hiring or contracting labor, which converts labor from the owner's sweat equity into real cash (already reflected in cashOutlayInclLaborUsd, which feeds launchLiquidityRequiredUsd for batch_50/batch_100) and into a real payroll/contractor-management problem this document does not solve.
  5. Build-to-order (50% deposit before cutting material) is still materially safer than build-to-stock, but "safer" is not "safe." Do not speculatively build inventory ahead of orders (killCriterion K1), and do not treat a favorable fundingGapIfDepositsCollectedUsd as license to skip the liability-insurance/registration/compliance gates in §9.

7. Batch size vs. annual demand — kept explicit, not conflated

Batch size (10/50/100) is a manufacturing scheduling decision — how many units get built in one production run, sharing one tooling investment. It is not a claim about how many units will sell in a year. A 50-unit batch could be built as one continuous run or as two 25-unit runs; that is a cash-flow and demand-pacing choice, not a different product.

Demand is a separate, currently unvalidated hypothesis (economics.json → demandHypothesis):

  • Hardware-only kit, Year 1: 8–20 units, hypothesis only — no landing page, no waitlist, no pre-order, no paid-ad test has been run for this SKU.
  • Donor-integration service, Year 1: 0–10 jobs, hypothesis only — zero inquiries received to date.

Before either range may be treated as a forecast rather than a guess, this project needs: a real deposit-collecting landing page run for 4–6 weeks; at least one paid, non-owner, arm's-length order at the tested price; and a documented community/forum interest signal (e.g. MAME/retro-arcade hobbyist threads) distinct from Mike's own posts. Even a validated signal would only establish demand, not an uncontested market — both offers compete with used/secondhand cabinets, independent repair shops, any licensed alternative that exists for an adjacent generation, and simply not buying anything (economics.json → demandHypothesis.competingAlternativesNote).

Standalone vs. sequential tooling, reconciled. Every scenario's own nreTotalUsd in §4–§5 is deliberately a standalone figure — what that batch size (or the donor job) would cost in tooling if it were the first and only commercial launch, so 10/50/100-unit batches and the donor job stay comparable on an apples-to-apples basis for break-even math. That is intentionally conservative for a single scenario's own break-even, but it is not what a real sequential 10-then-50-then-100 rollout actually costs in tooling. economics.py's sequential_rollout_tooling_usd(data, ["batch_10", "batch_50", "batch_100"]) shows the real number is ~$2,650 mid (batch_10 pays its full $1,300–2,900 standalone tooling; batch_50 pays only its $300–800 incremental packaging die-line, reusing batch_10's jigs; batch_100 pays $0 incremental) — against ~$7,400 mid if each of the three were actually launched as three independent standalone businesses. If Mike pursues the hardware-only kit and the donor-integration service together rather than as isolated standalone launches, the one-time launch-cost pool (~$2,950 mid) is likewise paid once, not once per offer — the standalone figures in §4/§6 are each scenario's number in isolation, deliberately conservative for comparison purposes, not a claim that running everything together multiplies the fixed-cost burden.

8. Manufacturing routing, batch QA, and production release

This is the process a real batch would follow — not yet executed, gated behind the conditions in §10.

8a. Production-release gate (before any tooling is cut)

  1. Convert the current concept CAD (cad/parameters.json, cad/generated/) from outline/schematic cutouts to a released drawing set: exact control-deck cutout dimensions for the chosen trackball/encoder/button hardware, pocket depths, tool-diameter-aware joinery, edge-banding allowance, fastener counterbores, and true sheet nesting (replacing the 55%-utilization placeholder with a real nesting-software or CNC-shop output).
  2. Dry-fit the released drawings against actually-purchased sample hardware (trackball, encoder, buttons) before cutting a CNC program — supplier mounting templates control the real cutout, not the nominal 76.2 mm ball diameter (per research/hardware-and-fabrication.md).
  3. Build and prove the drilling/routing jig and wiring-harness jig against that released drawing.

Nothing below this line should be committed to before step 1–3 are done and the prototype (§10) has passed its own build-acceptance gates.

8b. Routing (per unit, once released)

  1. Material prep — CNC-cut (or hand-routed via template for the prototype only) panels from released drawings; edge-band/seal all cut edges.
  2. Hardware install — mount trackball, encoder, and button panel into the released cutout; verify fastener torque and panel squareness.
  3. Wiring harness assembly — build and route the control harness using the wiring jig; label both ends of every run; crimp with proper tooling; leave service loops.
  4. Assembly / dry-fit / fasten — assemble the flat-pack shell using the demountable fastener system; confirm it can be disassembled and reassembled (stair-transport requirement carried from arcade/PLAN.md).
  5. Finish — sand with extraction, ease hand-contact edges, seal/paint/edge-trim per the chosen finish.
  6. Functional QA / test (batch QA, below).
  7. Packaging and labeling.

8c. Batch QA (per unit, before it is released to stock/shipment)

Carried forward from this project's own prototype acceptance targets (arcade/PLAN.md §7), scoped to what a hardware-only kit (no game) can actually test:

  • Trackball: slow rolls, diagonal rolls, and hard strokes in both axes — correct direction, no wrap/reversal/dropout, USB HID identity stable after reconnect.
  • Buttons: each button actuates cleanly, no missed/duplicate signal, panel labeling matches the wiring map.
  • Audio/power/ventilation: rated external supply present and correctly fused, no exposed conductors, fan/vent path clear.
  • Structural: squareness, fastener engagement, door/panel swing, no sharp edges, cable strain relief intact.
  • A written per-unit QA record (serial number, date, builder, pass/fail per check, corrective action if any) travels with the unit — this is also the serviceability record referenced in §8e.

A unit that fails any check is not packaged or counted toward the batch's sellable output; rework or scrap, logged, not silently waved through.

8d. Packaging and transport

  • Flat-pack panels ship disassembled, exploiting the demountable fastener design — this is what keeps the shipment within large-parcel/LTL range rather than requiring freight-class crating for an assembled ~950 mm-tall cabinet.
  • Packaging cost modeled at $35–70/unit (small-quantity corrugated + foam/corner-protector allowance, no bulk-quantity discount assumed — no quote supports one, kept flat across all batch sizes per the same conservative philosophy as material costs).
  • Freight-out modeled at $150–350/unit domestic, informed by 2026 LTL benchmarks ($100–600/pallet, Warp) for an estimated ~48–58 kg/106–128 lb flat-pack crate — corrected: actual Station A panel area (3.53 m²) × 18 mm thickness × an assumed 600 kg/m³ plywood density, plus a hardware/electronics/fastener/packaging allowance (economics.json → sharedAssumptions.estimatedShippedMass). An earlier draft's ~110 kg/245 lb figure derived mass from the full purchased-sheet area (3 sheets × ~2.98 m² ≈ 8.94 m²), which counts the 45%-waste offcuts from the nesting placeholder as if they were shipped — they are scrap, not product. Still route- and carrier-dependent, no account-specific quote obtained; confirm actual parcel-vs-LTL classification and negotiated rates before committing to retail pricing.
  • Donor-integration jobs are modeled with $0 packaging/freight — bespoke, high-value, fragile donor hardware is assumed to travel by local/regional delivery or customer pickup, not common carrier.

8e. Serviceability and warranty

  • 1-year limited hardware warranty on the furniture and included electronics (hardware-only kit); 5% of revenue reserved for parts + labor + return-freight claims (warrantyReservePct_hardwareOnly = 0.05, a common small-hardware-business planning convention, not derived from any claims history since none exists pre-launch).
  • Donor-integration jobs carry a higher 7% bailment/damage reserve instead of a product warranty, reflecting custody risk of the customer's own possibly-irreplaceable hardware, not a defect-repair obligation.
  • Every shipped unit's QA record (§8c) is the service baseline: a future repair starts from "what passed at build time," not guesswork.
  • Spare parts: the trackball and encoder are off-the-shelf Ultimarc parts (observed $79/$108 respectively) — a customer can source a replacement independently if we do not maintain spares inventory; this should be stated plainly in the warranty terms rather than implied.

10. Kill criteria and go/no-go

Go/no-go recommendation: Personal prototype only, at this time. This is an explicitly acceptable outcome of this analysis, not an incomplete deliverable — the corrected numbers do not currently clear a bar that beats the real, sourced, factory-licensed alternative for 2 of the 4 target games, and a bare hardware shell priced near a complete, warrantied, licensed factory cabinet (even one still in preorder) is a weak position regardless of production efficiency. The correction pass made this recommendation more conservative, not less: a previously-missing landed-cost allowance lowered every hardware-only margin, and correcting the donor-integration job's tooling from an amortized $90 to its real $900 standalone cost flipped that offer's batch-loaded economics from "the strongest number in this document" to negative at every tested stress level.

Conditions before commercial launch of the hardware-only kit (all required):

  1. Prototype built and passes arcade/PLAN.md's own build-acceptance gates (trackball/button/fit/safety tests).
  2. Three unrelated paid pilot orders at a price with positive stressed contribution, followed by successful fulfillment and support acceptance. Then apply K1's separate 10-unit funding gate; three pilots do not establish 50-unit demand.
  3. Liability/product insurance bound; applicable product-safety, electrical/EMC and certification requirements documented and completed for the released design with qualified advice (§9).
  4. A differentiation story beyond price that a complete factory cabinet cannot offer — multi-game flexibility for a customer's own legal ROM/donor library, custom furniture integration, or the classic (non-reboot) Silver Strike/legacy Big Buck gap — validated with a few real prospective buyers, not asserted.

Conditions before offering donor-integration commercially: signed bailment/liability waiver reviewed; liability insurance covering custody of third-party property bound; at least one real inquiry from someone who already owns or has a credible path to a legitimate donor system; a funding plan for the $900 standalone furniture-shell tooling investment (owner-funded up front, or pre-validated volume sufficient to amortize it) — do not rely on a single job's deposit to cover it.
