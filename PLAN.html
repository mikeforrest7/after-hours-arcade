Executive product and production presentation · Arcade experience specification · Production business plan

Recommendation

Design the custom option around two modular stations, not four duplicated commercial enclosures:

  1. A trackball pedestal with a separate 43-inch wall-mounted display, for Golden Tee and Silver Strike when their selected runtimes are proven.
  2. A two-player shooter station with a separate 55-inch display, supporting handheld optical guns for suitable games and reserving floor space for a separately engineered mounted-gun branch.

Use commodity displays, a reusable PC bench, off-the-shelf trackball/buttons and a plywood shell where a custom build earns its cost and fit. Compare licensed home products before choosing emulation or a complete legitimate donor. Buying complete cabinets may change this two-station layout; no factory system is assumed to fit inside the custom pods. Spend first on version-specific proof, then on cabinetry.

Commercial cabinets cost more than the bill of electronics: custom controls, software/content, durable fabrication, integration, distribution and support also contribute. We have no evidence establishing what fraction is licensing. The accessible saving is the enclosure, display and integration labor; reverse-engineering four commercial games from scratch is unlikely to be the cheapest path.

The plan includes all four requested games. None is silently replaced. Exact releases remain unselected. Classic Silver Strike donor integration and modern Jurassic Park remain difficult branches, but factory Silver Strike home products and licensed Big Buck alternatives must be considered alongside them. A cheap shell does not resolve software, controls or acquisition rights.

3. Technical architecture

Trackball station

  • Nominal furniture envelope 760 W × 610 D × 950 H mm (29.9 × 24.0 × 37.4 in). Validate height with a cardboard/full-size mock-up before cutting.
  • Start with a commercial 3-inch / 76.2 mm trackball. A cheap mouse trackball rarely reproduces the inertia, strike tolerance and speed range desired here.
  • PC/emulator branch: relative USB HID X/Y plus a separate button encoder. Disable unintended pointer acceleration; document the actual input provider. Choose sensitivity in-game rather than applying multiple hidden scaling layers.
  • Original Silver Strike branch: preserve its trackball encoder interface, I/O board and required buttons. A USB trackball is not automatically compatible with the donor board. Use interchangeable panels or a properly designed switch/interface, never connect one set of powered encoder outputs to two hosts in parallel.
  • Allow five game buttons plus separate deliberate service/menu/exit access. Golden Tee Fore and Silver Strike have different labels; use an unobstructed, replaceable label panel and per-game profiles.
  • Keep the hand's forward strike path clear. Wall-mounting the TV independently eliminates a cabinet monitor shelf in the immediate hand path, but the actual setback still needs a physical swing trial.

Shooter station

  • Nominal low console envelope 1,300 W × 400 D × 750 H mm (51.2 × 15.7 × 29.5 in). It is furniture/holster storage, not a freestanding TV support.
  • Handheld branch: two independently identified USB optical guns; pick OpenFIRE for an open-firmware DIY route or a supported commercial gun where reduced tuning work is worth the price. Try one unit before buying a matched pair.
  • Sinden's visible border, IR-marker systems' line of sight, camera field of view and room lighting are different installation constraints. A gun sold as USB does not prove compatibility with an arbitrary game runtime.
  • Mounted branch: retain original Jurassic guns/I/O or engineer two-axis analog assemblies only after proving their complete input contract. A USB HID MCU with ADC inputs can represent analog position for a compatible runtime; it does not automatically emulate RIO or make original game electronics accept the new device.
  • Recoil is a separate output/power subsystem. Baseline is no powered recoil or motion. If added, use documented parts, rated external supply, protected driver, flyback suppression where required, thermal/duty-cycle limits, guards and an accessible hardware disable. Do not power a solenoid from a USB logic rail or size a supply from fuse ratings alone.
  • Legacy Big Buck may use alternating players on a single gun. Two installed guns do not create a multiplayer mode the game lacks.

Compute and runtime

Bench first on an existing reasonably modern x86-64 PC. Procurement target for the emulation lane: a strong recent desktop CPU (e.g. Ryzen 5 5600 / Core i5-12400 class or better), 16 GB RAM and 500 GB–1 TB SSD. This is a benchmark candidate, not a guarantee. MAME can be CPU-bound; an expensive GPU does not make its emulated Voodoo hardware free. For Supermodel, verify the actual GPU/API requirements and performance before deciding whether integrated graphics suffice.

One PC per concurrently active emulation station is the boring reliable arrangement. One shared PC is cheaper only if one game at a time is acceptable. Do not casually run two emulators on one Windows desktop and assume focus, audio and USB routing will remain independent. Original donor computers may add further boxes; preserve ventilation and service space for each, and do not count them as the emulation PC.

Start games directly while proving inputs. Add a simple frontend only after each exact launch/exit path works. Freeze application versions, stable device identities, display modes and per-game configuration. MAME's mapdevice belongs in its controller configuration, not an arbitrary game/default file; its documentation also warns that not every input provider supplies stable unique IDs.

https://docs.mamedev.org/advanced/devicemap.html

Use local-only operation where the selected product legitimately supports it. Keep unsupported legacy donor operating systems off the household network unless a documented authorized service requires connectivity; segment them if needed. Do not patch authentication or redirect a game to a fabricated server to make it appear supported.

Use a normal OS shutdown button and accessible service keyboard. Do not use a switched power strip as the routine PC shutdown. Back up configuration/NVRAM and authorized system images according to their license; keep game media outside the source repository.

4. Display strategy: buy the panel, manufacture the furniture

Do not manufacture LCD/OLED panels. Fabricating the semiconductor backplane, optical stack and driver electronics is industrial production, not a cost-saving basement process. Build the bezel/frame/hood and buy a complete warranted TV or monitor.

A bare LCD panel plus controller, PSU, frame and protection is an optional salvage experiment. Exact panel/controller compatibility, backlight power, firmware, exposed conductors and mechanical fragility often erase the saving. The default is an intact off-the-shelf display with its original power enclosure retained.

Display Nominal 16:9 active width × height Proposed role
43-inch About 952 × 535 mm Trackball station
55-inch About 1,218 × 685 mm Shooter station

These are active-area calculations, not chassis/cutout dimensions. Measure the chosen unit's housing, VESA, cable bends, ventilation and bracket standoff. A 4:3 game is pillarboxed; never stretch it to fill 16:9. Bezel dimensions must also allow Sinden borders or IR emitters if that gun route is selected.

Display acceptance: correct refresh and aspect, overscan off, interpolation/noise processing off, game mode on, repeatable power/input restoration, no distracting dimming and tested input latency. Start with 60 Hz output matching the title; 4K resolution or 120 Hz marketing alone does not prove low latency. The whole input-to-photon chain matters.

For optical guns, viewing distance follows the camera lens and complete target geometry, not a generic TV-distance rule. The illustrative CAD reserves a test zone; acceptance must establish a near/far boundary with full corner coverage for both players. Measure with room lights and any daylight conditions expected in use.

5. Manufacturing and installation sequence

  1. Full-size mock-up. Cardboard or scrap top; real trackball housing; borrowed display; player grip and strike tests. Tape station and stance outlines on the floor. This is cheaper than recutting finished plywood.
  2. Measure the purchased parts. Record material thickness with calipers. Supplier mounting drawings control trackball, buttons and holsters. The nominal 76.2 mm ball is not a cutout diameter. Determine service and connector clearances.
  3. Release fabrication CAD. Add joints, fasteners, door hinges, cable holes, removable panels, edge radii, material grain, tool diameter, pocket depths, tolerances and assembly labels. Generate exact quantities and optimized nesting only after those decisions. The delivered DXF contains panel blanks, not this release.
  4. Make the panels. 18 mm good-quality plywood is a useful starting assumption. Hand route with templates/straightedges or send released DXF/STEP drawings to a local CNC shop. CNC can improve repeatability, but setup/cutting/finishing quotes must be compared with buying a flat-pack. MDF is heavy and more vulnerable to a damp basement; protect all edges if chosen.
  5. Dry-fit and prove access. Check squareness, screw/insert positions, door swing, service reach and cable strain relief. Split furniture into manageable modules. Check the longest panel and the TV carton through stair turns; cabinet width alone is insufficient.
  6. Finish. Sand with extraction and suitable PPE; ease hand-contact edges; prime/seal cut edges; paint, laminate or apply original artwork. Keep solvents/dust away from assembled electronics. Allow the actual finish's cure time.
  7. Low-voltage assembly. Label both ends of every harness, use keyed connectors and proper crimp tooling, preserve ground/voltage separation, protect wire exits and leave service loops. Keep power and data routing deliberate. Do not hot-switch raw donor connections unless the interface is designed for it.
  8. Install the displays independently. Rated commercial brackets fixed to verified structure by a competent installer. No drywall-only mounting, decorative plywood TV support or unverified DIY tip-over calculation. Anchor furniture where needed for energetic trackball play and children.
  9. Commission. Test each subsystem before attaching expensive components; then complete the final acceptance tests. Keep as-built drawings and connector/pinout records with the machine.

Ventilation design follows measured heat, not a fan decoration: clear intake/exhaust paths, filters that can be cleaned, separated compartments where needed, and service access. Electronics stay above plausible floor-water exposure. Stop use on condensation or water damage. Have an electrician confirm circuit capacity, grounding and applicable basement GFCI requirements. No exposed mains, daisy-chained power strips, CRT repairs or mains rewiring are part of the DIY scope. Maintain smoke detection and unobstructed escape routes.

More people writing simultaneous game profiles or cabinet dimensions adds conflict without resolving missing hardware. Do not run repeated agent passes over an already analyzed binary; retain evidence and send bounded questions.

The included graph query reports a theoretical dependency-path estimate and total person-hours. These exclude parts lead times, human approval waits, shared-builder contention and new game-reimplementation work. A reasonable planning allowance is 6–10 weekends after game feasibility and parts acquisition, not a guarantee. Unproven modern runtime development has no credible completion date yet.

7. Acceptance gates

Targets below are proposed engineering acceptance criteria, not measured results or manufacturer promises. Adjust explicitly at G0, then hold them fixed for comparison.

Area Test and pass condition
All four game identities Exact selected build shown; required course/game/mission completed. Substitutions require a recorded change to the requirement.
Trackball Slow rolls, diagonal rolls and 20 hard strokes show correct direction and no wrap/reversal/dropout; gentle putts and hard shots remain distinguishable. No hand contact with display, buttons or sharp edges.
Buttons 100 deliberate actuations per critical button without missed or duplicate game actions; combinations needed by the game work. Service/exit cannot be triggered accidentally by ordinary play.
Optical aim Nine-point grid at near/far accepted player positions, ten shots per point/player: at least 95% within 2% of active display width from intended point. This is a design target; verify that the selected game/test tool exposes adequate coordinates. No player cross-control.
Positional aim Smooth monotonic sweep to each limit, repeatable neutral, no jitter or dead region hiding a target; recheck after warm-up and restart. Physical stops protect pots and wiring.
Reload/secondary Big Buck pump/trigger order works at realistic cadence; Jurassic-specific secondary functions verified rather than assuming a pump.
Speed and latency Maintain emulated real-time speed with sound during representative demanding scenes; record dropped frames/stutter. Provisional total input-to-photon target ≤50 ms; measure with high-speed video or an instrumented method, not a TV's advertised pixel response.
Restart/device identity Ten cold boots and planned reconnect scenarios retain P1/P2 identity, calibration, settings and audio. Missing devices fail visibly, not by controlling another player.
Thermal/endurance Four-hour mixed-game session at expected room conditions; no crash, input loss, throttling or temperatures exceeding component specifications. Report hottest readings and ambient temperature.
Safety/fit Competent check of TV mounting, furniture stability, cable pulls, guarded moving parts, rated power hardware, grounding/GFCI where required, ventilation and egress; physical access route proven.
Recovery Restore configuration to a spare/test location from backup, prove a chosen game starts with correct controls, and record the process. No reliance on an irreplaceable undocumented setup.

Maintain a short failure log: symptom, exact version/hardware, reproduction, evidence, owner, correction and retest. Do not loosen a target just to turn a failed bench into a procurement approval.

8. Decisions Mike owns; work the agent can own

Mike decides: exact release for each game; whether original feel or only gameplay matters; maximum total spend including donor systems; acceptable room footprint; handheld versus mounted controls; whether any later recoil/motion or online feature is worth its cost. Purchases and external communications require their own exact authorization.

The agent can own: primary-source compatibility checks, BOM reconciliation, CAD revisions from actual measurements, graph validation/change impact, approved local software setup and repeatable test execution. A builder/installer owns physical measurements and safety work that cannot be observed remotely.

Nothing needs to be bought to make the next decision. First identify the four exact arcade versions from names/photos and measure the room/access route. Then prove the two control families on a bench. Release furniture for cutting only after the software/control choices survive that test.
